Supermarket

Does a better-informed shopper choose a faster line?

Explore queue balancing, imperfect information, and the price of finding out. Change one store’s policy. Follow the same shoppers through both.

Four lanes in each store. Total exit time includes every step from arrival to departure.

1. Set up2. Watch3. Compare4. Test again

Experimental teaching lab · review proposal. The baseline uses nearby people counts. The experiment can inspect workloads or reserve an express lane. Demand stays fixed, shoppers never switch queues, and time is in abstract units. These are model assumptions, not a forecast for a real supermarket.

Try a comparison

  1. Choose a scenario or edit the experiment settings and apply them.
  2. Step through time, Run, or Finish comparison. Both stores receive the same arrivals and baskets.
  3. Compare total exit time, including search and travel. Let every shopper finish before comparing final averages.

Time is measured in abstract units, not minutes. Each basket takes its number of items plus three units to check out. Shoppers choose once and stay in their queue.

One seed is one example

Does the result hold across twelve seeds?

Rerun the applied settings with twelve consecutive seeds, starting at the applied seed. Each trial pairs identical shoppers across stores and drains every queue. Your main comparison stays where you left it.

Each nonempty seed gets equal weight in the summary. Empty trials have no mean and are excluded. This deterministic sensitivity check is not a confidence interval or a probability that a policy is best. Changing settings clears these results.

Optional guided experiments

Each question loads its own fixed scenario. Finish the comparison, choose an answer, and check. These examples do not establish a universal best policy.
